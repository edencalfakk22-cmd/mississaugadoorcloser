Half the wedges we pull out from under Mississauga fire doors are there because the door genuinely needs to stand open — a busy condo corridor to the garage, a service route in a warehouse, a school or clinic doorway with carts going through all day. The compliant way to do that is an electromagnetic hold-open, either a wall or floor magnet paired with the closer or a closer-holder unit, wired so the door releases and self-closes the moment the fire alarm signals. We install them, tie them into the alarm circuit through your alarm contractor, and test the release so you can stop losing the argument with your inspector.

The other half of this work is powered access — low-energy swing operators with push plates or wave sensors at accessible entrances, common in Mississauga condos, medical offices and municipal buildings. They fail in familiar ways: a dead or unpaired transmitter in the push plate, an operator that has lost its open and close timing, an arm that binds because the door has dropped, or an operator quietly fighting a badly set manual closer underneath it. We service the operator, the actuators and the door itself together, and confirm the door still closes and latches properly when the power is off, because that is when it matters most.

FAQs

Hold-Opens & Automatic Door Operators — your questions

Can we legally hold a fire door open in our condo corridor?

Yes, with the right device. A hold-open wired to the fire alarm, which releases the door on a signal, is exactly how a corridor door is meant to stand open. A wedge, a hook, a strap or a mechanical hold-open arm is not, because nothing releases any of them when it matters.

Our push-plate operator has stopped responding. Is that the plate or the operator?

It could be either, and it is worth finding out rather than replacing the whole assembly. Wireless actuators fail on flat batteries, hard-wired plates fail at the switch or in the wiring, and the operator itself fails at the control board or a worn arm. We test the chain from the plate through to the operator and repair the part that has actually failed.

Does an automatic operator mean we no longer need a working closer?

No, and that assumption causes a lot of failures. When the power goes or a board dies, the door falls back on whatever the closer behind it is set at. If that has been left slack because the operator normally does the work, you are left with a rated door that will not latch, or an entrance nobody can move. We set the manual behaviour deliberately.
